# Diabetes basics
Source: WHO Diabetes fact sheet; NIDDK "What Is Diabetes?" | https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/diabetes

## What diabetes is
Diabetes is a chronic disease in which blood glucose (blood sugar) stays too high. It happens when the pancreas does not make enough insulin, or when the body cannot use insulin effectively (insulin resistance). Insulin is the hormone that moves glucose from the blood into cells for energy. Over time, high blood glucose damages blood vessels and nerves.

## Types of diabetes
Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune condition in which the body makes little or no insulin; it is often diagnosed in children and young adults and requires insulin treatment. Type 2 diabetes is the most common form (about 9 in 10 cases); the body becomes resistant to insulin and gradually makes less of it. It is strongly linked to excess body weight and physical inactivity, and often develops slowly over years. Gestational diabetes is high blood glucose that first appears during pregnancy. Prediabetes means blood glucose is higher than normal but not yet high enough for a type 2 diabetes diagnosis.

## How common diabetes is
According to WHO, the number of adults living with diabetes rose from about 200 million in 1990 to about 830 million in 2022, and prevalence has been rising faster in low- and middle-income countries. More than half of adults with diabetes were not taking medication for it.

## Symptoms
Common symptoms of high blood glucose include frequent urination, increased thirst, increased hunger, tiredness, blurred vision, slow-healing cuts or sores, frequent infections, numbness or tingling in the hands or feet, and unexplained weight loss (more typical of type 1). Many people with type 2 diabetes or prediabetes have no symptoms for years, which is why testing matters.

## Complications
Long-term high blood glucose raises the risk of heart attack and stroke, kidney disease (nephropathy), eye damage that can lead to vision loss (retinopathy), nerve damage (neuropathy), foot ulcers and lower-limb amputation, gum disease, and problems in pregnancy. Keeping glucose, blood pressure and cholesterol in target ranges lowers these risks.

## Emergency warning signs
Seek urgent medical care for very high blood glucose with vomiting, abdominal pain, confusion, fruity-smelling breath or rapid breathing, which can signal diabetic ketoacidosis. Very low blood glucose (below 70 mg/dL) with shakiness, sweating, confusion or fainting also needs immediate treatment.
