# Diagnosing diabetes and reading glucose results
Source: American Diabetes Association, Standards of Care in Diabetes, section 2 "Diagnosis and Classification" | https://diabetesjournals.org/care/issue/48/Supplement_1

## Diagnostic thresholds
The American Diabetes Association diagnoses diabetes with any of these results: fasting plasma glucose of 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher after at least 8 hours without food; 2-hour plasma glucose of 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or higher during a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT); HbA1c (A1C) of 6.5% or higher; or a random plasma glucose of 200 mg/dL or higher in a person with classic symptoms of high blood glucose. Unless symptoms are clear, a diagnosis needs two abnormal results, either from the same sample or from two separate tests.

## Prediabetes ranges
Prediabetes is defined by a fasting plasma glucose of 100 to 125 mg/dL (impaired fasting glucose), a 2-hour OGTT glucose of 140 to 199 mg/dL (impaired glucose tolerance), or an HbA1c of 5.7% to 6.4%. People with prediabetes have a much higher chance of developing type 2 diabetes, heart disease and stroke.

## Normal ranges
Normal values are a fasting plasma glucose below 100 mg/dL, a 2-hour OGTT glucose below 140 mg/dL, and an HbA1c below 5.7%. Blood glucose below 70 mg/dL is considered low (hypoglycemia).

## The oral glucose tolerance test
In an oral glucose tolerance test the person fasts overnight, drinks a solution containing 75 g of glucose, and has blood glucose measured 2 hours later. The Glucose value in this app's dataset is this 2-hour OGTT result, so it should be compared with the OGTT ranges: below 140 mg/dL is normal, 140 to 199 mg/dL suggests impaired glucose tolerance (prediabetes), and 200 mg/dL or higher is in the diabetes range.

## HbA1c
HbA1c (glycated hemoglobin, A1C) reflects average blood glucose over roughly the past 2 to 3 months and does not require fasting. It can be less reliable in conditions that change red blood cell turnover, such as anemia, pregnancy, recent blood loss or some hemoglobin variants.

## Who should be tested
The ADA recommends screening all adults starting at age 35, and testing adults of any age who are overweight or obese (BMI 25 or higher, or 23 or higher in Asian American people) and have one or more additional risk factors. People with prediabetes should be retested at least once a year, and women who had gestational diabetes should be tested at least every 3 years for life.
